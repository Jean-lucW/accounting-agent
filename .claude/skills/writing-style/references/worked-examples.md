# Worked examples

Read this to calibrate the voice, especially before a document type you have not written before. The full policy shows the structure end to end. The pairs show the specific moves that turn template prose into house voice.

Everything here is about the fictional Example Group in `config/group.example.toml` (HoldCo, OpCo US, OpCo EU). The people are roles, not names.

---

## A full policy in house style

Around 400 words. Note the unheaded opener, the local-requirements paragraph closing it, `###` headings in Title Case, one bold sub-heading, bullets without full stops, one bold sentence carrying the section's principle, and a short close before Review.

> This policy covers how staff spend Example Group's money on cards and get it into the books. It applies to everyone who holds a company card in HoldCo, OpCo US or OpCo EU.
>
> Where the law of a country an entity operates in sets a different or stricter requirement, that requirement applies in that entity and this policy is read with it.
>
> ### Receipts
>
> Every card charge needs its receipt in the accounting inbox or the Slack app within 2 working days. The bank feed shows the charge the next morning, and a charge with no receipt is a line the bookkeeper has to chase. A photo of a paper receipt is fine if the supplier, the amount and the currency can be read.
>
> **If you have lost a receipt, say so the same day and say what the charge was for.**
>
> ### Which Entity Pays
>
> Use the card of the entity you work for, even when the cost is for another entity. The bookkeeper moves the cost across between the entities each month, so nothing is lost by paying from the wrong card. Where a supplier only bills one entity, tell the bookkeeper which entity bears the cost when you send the receipt. Your line manager approves anything over 500 GBP or local equivalent before you pay.
>
> Subscriptions go through the controller. Most of what looks like a new tool is already paid for somewhere in the group.
>
> ### Travel
>
> Book through the travel tool where it covers the route. Flights under 5 hours are Economy. Hotels up to 200 GBP per night or local equivalent need no approval; above that, agree it with your line manager before you book. Breakfast comes with the hotel, not the meal allowance.
>
> ### What Is Not Reimbursable
>
> - Upgrades bought for personal comfort
> - Fines and penalty charges, including parking
> - Anything already paid for by another entity's card
>
> ### Review
>
> This policy is reviewed every 12 months, and sooner when the group's entities, systems or approval limits change.

What that document does not do: no callouts, no summary paragraph at the end, no invented approval committee, no "we recognise that flexibility is important to our people".

## Before and after

### 1. Template prose with no rule under it

Before:

> Finance activities will be aligned with the strategic objectives and needs of the organisation. The finance team will collaborate with all managers to aggregate reporting needs and prioritise them based on organisational objectives.

Nothing there states a view, gives a reason, names a carve-out, or ends short. There is no rule to preserve, so the rewrite has to decide what the rule actually is.

After:

> Each entity's books close on the third working day of the month. The management accounts go to the finance director on the fifth, so anything posted later lands in next month's pack. A late supplier invoice is accrued rather than left out. Send month-end questions to **[owner to be named]** by the second working day.

### 2. Wrong-organisation language

Before:

> Employees must protect confidential customer information at all times. Any breach could damage our reputation with customers and undermine the trust our clients place in us.

HoldCo has no customers. The real reason sits one layer down: the group's own bank details and its supplier relationships.

After:

> Bank details and payment approvals do not leave the finance team. Anyone who knows how a payment is approved can fake an invoice that passes, and the money is gone the same day. That includes screenshots of the bank portal and supplier bank details pasted into a chat. **Once a payment has gone we cannot get it back.**

### 3. Negation set up to be knocked down

Before:

> The supplier's name is not the test. What matters is which entity the invoice is addressed to and which entity bears the cost.

The first sentence exists only to be corrected by the second. Write the second and give it the scope it needs.

After:

> A cost is booked in the entity that bears it, which is usually the entity the invoice is addressed to; where the two differ, `rules/GROUP.md` decides and the invoice is never booked from the supplier's name alone.

### 4. Unnamed authority and an asserted control

Before:

> All Xero user access is reviewed on a quarterly basis by senior management, and any exceptions are escalated to the appropriate governance forum.

Nobody is named and neither the review nor the forum exists. Papering over that is how a policy becomes something no one can act on.

After:

> Adviser access to an entity's Xero organisation is granted by **[owner to be named]** and is removed when the reason for it ends. There is no quarterly review cycle. This control is not yet built, and until it is, removal depends on the person who asked for the access saying when they are done with it.

### 5. Announced clarity and a limit that reads like a target

Before:

> The guiding principle is simple: employees should endeavour to spend company money responsibly, and where appropriate, seek approval in advance. Hotel spend should not exceed $300 per night.

Three failures in two sentences: the drumroll colon, two hedges doing the work a decision should do, and a currency symbol.

After:

> **The guiding principle is that you are expected to spend the company's money as if it were your own.** The limits below are ceilings, not targets. Hotels up to 300 USD per night or local equivalent need no approval. Above that, agree it with your line manager before you book.

### 6. The tacked-on corrective

Before:

> Agree it with **[owner to be named]** before you sign, not after. Tell your line manager in advance rather than after the event, and get the change agreed before anything moves rather than once it has.

The sentence said "before" three times and then corrected an imagined reader three times. Every tail can go without losing a word of the rule.

After:

> Agree it with **[owner to be named]** before you sign. Tell your line manager in advance, and get the change agreed before anything moves.

### 7. The policy explaining why it exists

Before:

> This is a named policy in the auditor's file and is required under our banking covenants. It sets out the standards expected of all employees in relation to the reporting of payment errors.

The reader does not act on either sentence. Where the requirement is operative, it creates a duty with a deadline, so write the duty.

After:

> This policy covers what to do when a payment goes to the wrong account, goes twice, or nearly does. It applies to everyone in Example Group who can approve a payment.
>
> Some errors carry a notification duty. Where a payment error breaches a bank covenant, the group tells the bank within 5 working days, and **[owner to be named]** makes that notification. Your job is to report it internally the same day so that clock can start.
